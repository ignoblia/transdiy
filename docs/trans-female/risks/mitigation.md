# Risk Mitigation Strategies

This page outlines practical steps to minimise the risks associated with feminising HRT. Not all risks can be eliminated, but most can be significantly reduced.

## 1. Choose the Safest Route of Estrogen

| Route | VTE Risk | Recommendation |
|-------|----------|---------------|
| Transdermal (patches/gel) | Lowest | ✅ **Preferred by guidelines** |
| Injectable | Low | ✅ Good alternative |
| Sublingual | Moderate | ⚠️ Acceptable with monitoring |
| Oral (swallowed) | Highest | ❌ Avoid if risk factors present |

::: tip If you must use oral estrogen
- Take it sublingually (under the tongue) when possible — this reduces first-pass metabolism
- Keep the dose as low as effective
- Do not smoke
- Monitor blood pressure and lipids regularly
:::

## 2. Use the Lowest Effective Anti-Androgen Dose

| Anti-Androgen | Maximum Safe Dose | Notes |
|---------------|-------------------|-------|
| Spironolactone | 200 mg/day | Monitor potassium |
| CPA | **10–12.5 mg/day** | Higher doses increase meningioma risk |
| Bicalutamide | 50 mg/day | Monitor liver function |

## 3. Lifestyle Modifications

### Smoking

::: danger Smoking + estrogen = significantly increased VTE risk
If you smoke and take oral estrogen, your VTE risk is **multiplicative** — much higher than either risk alone. Quitting smoking is the single most impactful thing you can do to reduce HRT risks.
:::

### Weight Management

- Obesity (BMI > 30) independently increases VTE risk
- Maintain a healthy weight through diet and exercise
- Even modest weight loss (5–10%) reduces risk

### Exercise

- 150 minutes moderate cardio per week
- Strength training 2×/week
- Walking, swimming, cycling are all excellent options
- Reduces cardiovascular risk and improves mental health

### Hydration and Electrolytes (Spiro Users)

- Drink plenty of water
- Increase salt intake (spiro causes sodium loss)
- Avoid potassium supplements and high-potassium salt substitutes

## 4. Regular Monitoring

| Test | Frequency | Why |
|------|-----------|-----|
| Blood pressure | Every 1–3 months | Estrogen can raise BP |
| E2 + T | Every 3 months (first year) | Confirm levels in target range |
| CBC (complete blood count) | Every 3–6 months | Check for anaemia, etc. |
| Comprehensive metabolic panel | Every 6–12 months | Liver/kidney function, electrolytes |
| Lipid panel | Annually | Cholesterol, triglycerides |
| Prolactin (if on CPA) | Annually | Monitor for prolactin elevation |
| Potassium (if on spiro) | Every 3 months (year 1), then annually | Monitor for hyperkalemia |

## 5. Know Your Family History

Ask your family about:

- Blood clots (DVT, PE)
- Clotting disorders (Factor V Leiden, etc.)
- Breast cancer
- Heart disease (early onset)
- Meningioma

If you have a family history of any of these, discuss with a provider before starting HRT.

## 6. Emergency Preparedness

- Keep a list of your medications and doses
- Know the emergency signs (see "When to Stop / Seek Help" page)
- Have a backup supply of medication
- If DIY, have a plan to access medical care if needed
- Share your HRT status with a trusted friend who can advocate for you

## 7. Birth Control (If Needed)

::: warning HRT is NOT contraception
If you have a partner who can get you pregnant, use reliable contraception. Estrogen and anti-androgens do **not** reliably prevent pregnancy.
:::

## 8. Mental Health Monitoring

- HRT improves mental health for most trans people, but can worsen mood temporarily
- Monitor your mental health weekly (simple 1–10 scale)
- Have a therapist or support system
- If you have a history of depression, CPA may not be the best anti-androgen choice
