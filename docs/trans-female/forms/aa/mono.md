# Estrogen Monotherapy (E Alone)

## What Is Monotherapy?

Estrogen monotherapy means using **estradiol alone** to suppress testosterone, without adding an anti-androgen (spironolactone, bicalutamide, CPA, etc.). The goal is to keep E2 levels high enough that the HPG axis naturally reduces LH/FSH and therefore testosterone production.

## How It Works

1. High levels of estradiol in the bloodstream signal the hypothalamus/pituitary that there is enough sex hormone
2. The pituitary reduces LH and FSH output
3. The testes receive less LH stimulation
4. Testosterone production decreases

This is the same negative-feedback mechanism that regulates hormone production in cisgender people.

## Who Can Use Monotherapy?

Monotherapy is **not effective for everyone**. Success depends on:

- **Route** — injectable or high-dose transdermal estradiol works best; oral monotherapy is **not recommended**
- **Individual biology** — some people's HPG axes are more sensitive to E2 feedback than others
- **Dose** — sufficient E2 levels must be maintained at trough

## Dosing for Monotherapy

| Route | Dose | Typical Trough E2 |
|-------|------|-------------------|
| EV injections | 4–8 mg every 5 days | 200–300 pg/mL |
| EEn injections | 4–8 mg every 7 days | 200–300 pg/mL |
| EC injections | 3–6 mg every 7 days | 200–300 pg/mL |
| Transdermal patches | 200–300 mcg/24h | 150–250 pg/mL |
| Gel | 6–8 pumps/day | 150–250 pg/mL |

> Recent evidence (2024-2025 multisite studies) shows that most people achieve T suppression at the lower end of these ranges. Median effective weekly doses are around 4 mg (IQR 3-6 mg). Starting at 3-5 mg weekly and titrating up if needed is recommended to avoid supraphysiologic E2 levels.

::: danger Do NOT attempt monotherapy with oral pills
Oral estradiol at doses high enough to suppress T carries a significantly elevated VTE (blood clot) risk due to first-pass liver metabolism. Monotherapy should only be attempted with injectable or high-dose transdermal estradiol.
:::

## Effectiveness

- **~80% or more** of trans women achieve T < 50 ng/dL on injectable monotherapy (based on recent multisite studies)
- Some still need a low-dose anti-androgen even with adequate E2
- People with higher baseline T may need higher E2 doses
- Recent evidence suggests spironolactone may not provide additional T suppression when combined with injectable E2, though it can still be useful for blocking androgen receptors

## Pros & Cons

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| No anti-androgen side effects | High E2 doses needed |
| No spiro (potassium, urination) | May not work for everyone |
| No CPA (depression, meningioma risk) | Requires injections (typically) |
| No bica (liver monitoring) | More expensive (higher E2 dose) |
| Simpler regimen | Some people feel worse on very high E2 |
| No anti-androgen cost | |

## Monitoring

| Test | Target | Frequency |
|------|--------|-----------|
| E2 (trough) | 200–300 pg/mL (for monotherapy) | Every 3 months |
| T (trough) | < 50 ng/dL | Every 3 months |
| SHBG | < 150 nmol/L (may indicate too high E2) | Every 6 months |
| Blood pressure | Normal | Every visit |

::: tip Trough timing
For monotherapy, measuring E2 at trough (just before next injection) is critical. Peak E2 levels can be 500–1000+ pg/mL, which is fine — the concern is whether your trough is high enough to suppress T.
:::

## When to Add an Anti-Androgen

Consider adding a low-dose anti-androgen if:

- T > 50 ng/dL after 8–12 weeks on high-dose monotherapy
- SHBG is very high (>150 nmol/L) suggesting E2 dose can't go higher
- You cannot tolerate high E2 doses (mood effects, etc.)
- You are on a route that cannot achieve monotherapy-level E2 (pills, low-dose gel)
