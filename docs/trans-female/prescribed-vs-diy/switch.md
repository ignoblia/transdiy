# Switching from DIY to Prescribed HRT

## Why Switch?

Many trans women start on DIY HRT due to waiting lists, cost, or gatekeeping — and later transition to prescribed care when it becomes accessible. Reasons to switch include:

- **Access to monitoring** — regular blood tests and professional oversight
- **Legal protection** — prescribed medication is legal to possess and import
- **Quality assurance** — pharmaceutical-grade medication from regulated sources
- **Insurance coverage** — significantly lower ongoing costs
- **Long-term safety** — monitoring for rare side effects

## When to Switch

The best time to switch is when you have **stable, safe hormone levels** and a provider you trust. Ideally:

- Your E2 is 100–200 pg/mL
- Your T is below 50 ng/dL
- Your blood pressure is normal
- You have no active side effects requiring intervention

## How to Switch: Step by Step

### Step 1: Find a Trans-Friendly Provider

| Route | How to Find |
|-------|-------------|
| Informed consent clinic | Planned Parenthood (US), LGBTQ+ community health centres |
| Private gender specialist | Search WPATH provider directory; ask in trans community forums |
| GP referral | Ask your GP for a referral to an endocrinologist experienced in trans care |
| Telehealth | Services like Plume (US), GenderGP (UK/EU), or TransClinique (Australia) |

### Step 2: Prepare Your Medical History

Your new provider will want to know:

- **How long you've been on HRT**
- **What medications and doses** you take (be specific — brand, ester, dose, frequency)
- **Your last blood test results** (bring a copy if you have them)
- **Any side effects or issues** you've experienced
- **Your goals** — target levels, desired changes, surgical plans

::: tip Be honest with your provider
Providers who specialise in trans care generally understand why people DIY. Being honest about your history helps them provide better care. Most will not judge you — they see DIY patients regularly.
:::

### Step 3: Get Baseline Blood Tests

Your provider will likely order:

- E2 (estradiol)
- Total T (testosterone)
- SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin)
- CBC (complete blood count)
- Comprehensive metabolic panel (liver and kidney function)
- Lipid panel
- Prolactin (especially if on CPA)
- Blood pressure

### Step 4: Transition Your Prescription

Your provider may:

- **Continue your current regimen** if it's working and safe
- **Adjust doses** based on their target ranges and your blood results
- **Switch formulations** — e.g., from injectable DIY estradiol to prescribed patches or gel
- **Add monitoring** — prolactin checks if on CPA, potassium checks if on spironolactone

### Step 5: Dispose of Unused DIY Medication

Once you have a prescription:

| Situation | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| Unopened vials from trusted sources | Consider giving to a friend who still DIYs |
| Unopened vials from untrusted sources | Discard (not worth the risk) |
| Opened vials (injectables) | These have a limited shelf life once opened — check the vial |
| Expired medication | Discard properly |

## Potential Challenges

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| Provider refuses to match your DIY dose | Ask for their rationale; get a second opinion if needed |
| Provider insists on outdated protocols (e.g., 50+ mg CPA) | Educate them with WPATH SOC8 guidelines, or find another provider |
| Provider requires you to stop HRT before prescribing | This is a **red flag** — find a different provider |
| Insurance won't cover your DIY-established dose | Ask about formulary alternatives that match your needs |
| No informed consent available in your country | Continuing DIY alongside private blood monitoring may be your only option |

## What If You Can't Fully Switch?

For many trans women, the best option is a **hybrid approach**:

- **DIY for medications** (to save cost or access specific formulations)
- **GP for blood tests** (many GPs will order labs even if they won't prescribe)
- **Private blood tests** as a fallback

This approach is common in the UK, where NHS waiting lists are extremely long but GPs can order blood tests under a "shared care" or "harm reduction" arrangement.
