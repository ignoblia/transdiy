# Prescribed HRT (Trans Female)

## What Is Prescribed HRT?

Prescribed HRT means obtaining estrogen, anti-androgens, and progesterone through a licensed healthcare provider — typically an endocrinologist, primary care physician, or specialised gender clinic. Your care is medically supervised with regular blood tests and dose adjustments.

## Routes to Access

### Informed Consent Model

Under informed consent, you sign a form acknowledging the risks and benefits of HRT, and treatment begins without requiring a therapist's letter or formal diagnosis of gender dysphoria.

- **Common in:** United States (Planned Parenthood, many LGBTQ+ clinics), Australia (certain clinics), parts of Europe
- **Pros:** Fastest path to prescribed care; no gatekeeping
- **Cons:** Not available everywhere; some providers still require a letter

### Traditional Model (Therapist Letter Required)

You obtain a diagnosis of gender dysphoria from a mental health professional, who then provides a letter recommending HRT.

- **Common in:** Many European countries, UK (NHS Gender Identity Clinics), parts of Asia
- **Pros:** Structured care; may include counselling support
- **Cons:** Long waiting lists (up to 5–7 years in some countries), potential for gatekeeping

### Private Healthcare

- **Common in:** UK (private gender clinics), US (out-of-pocket), countries with limited public options
- **Pros:** Shorter waiting times; more choice of providers
- **Cons:** Expensive; costs vary widely

## Common Prescribed Regimens

| Regimen | Typical Prescription |
|---------|---------------------|
| Oral estradiol | 2–8 mg/day (often split 2× daily) |
| Transdermal patches | 50–200 mcg/24h, changed twice weekly |
| Estradiol gel | 1–4 pumps/day (Oestrogel, Sandrena) |
| Injectable EV | 2–8 mg every 5 days |
| Injectable EC | 1–5 mg every 7 days |
| Spironolactone | 50–200 mg/day (often 100 mg) |
| Cyproterone acetate | 10–12.5 mg/day |
| Finasteride | 1–5 mg/day (for hair preservation) |

## Pros of Prescribed HRT

| Benefit | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Medical supervision** | Regular blood tests and professional dose adjustments |
| **Quality-controlled medication** | Pharmaceutical-grade products from regulated pharmacies |
| **Insurance coverage** | Many insurance plans cover HRT partially or fully |
| **Legal protection** | Prescribed HRT is legal and recognised by medical authorities |
| **Monitoring for side effects** | Blood pressure, liver function, prolactin, and other markers tracked |
| **Access to additional care** | Referrals for voice training, hair removal, surgery if needed |

## Cons of Prescribed HRT

| Drawback | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Long waiting lists** | Up to 5–7 years in some countries (especially UK NHS) |
| **Gatekeeping** | Some providers impose unnecessary barriers or have outdated protocols |
| **Inflexible dosages** | Some providers are unwilling to prescribe higher doses or specific medications |
| **Limited medication options** | Some countries restrict injectable estradiol or certain anti-androgens |
| **Cost (private)** | Can be expensive without insurance coverage |
| **Pathologisation** | Being treated as having a "disorder" can feel dysphoric |
| **Outdated protocols** | Some providers still use high-dose CPA (50+ mg/day) or refuse transdermal options |

## Questions to Ask a Potential Provider

- What is your experience treating trans patients?
- Do you use the informed consent model or require a therapist letter?
- What are your target hormone ranges (E2, T)?
- Are you willing to prescribe injectable estradiol or transdermal routes?
- Do you monitor prolactin, blood pressure, and liver function?
- Are you familiar with WPATH SOC8 guidelines?

## Red Flags in a Provider

- Insists on using birth control pills (ethinylestradiol) instead of bioidentical estradiol
- Refuses to prescribe anti-androgens
- Targets testosterone levels above 50 ng/dL
- Requires RLE (real-life experience) before prescribing
- Unwilling to learn or consult guidelines
