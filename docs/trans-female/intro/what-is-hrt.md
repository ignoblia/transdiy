# What is HRT? (Trans Female Guide)

**HRT** stands for **Hormone Replacement Therapy**. For trans females, it is a medical treatment that helps align your physical characteristics with your gender identity through the use of hormones such as **estrogen**, **testosterone blockers**, and **progesterone**.

## How HRT Works

Gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) for trans women typically involves three steps:

1. **Suppressing testosterone** – Reducing your body's natural testosterone production to minimise masculine traits.
2. **Introducing estrogen** – Taking estradiol to promote feminine physical changes.
3. **Introducing progesterone** – Optionally added later to further support feminine development, particularly breast maturation.

Estradiol serves two important roles: it directly promotes feminine development, and at sufficient doses it can suppress testosterone on its own.

Progesterone is not strictly necessary, but some trans women find that adding it:
- Helps round out breast shape (Tanner stage 4/5 development)
- May improve libido, mood, and sleep quality
- May reduce anxiety

Evidence for progesterone's benefits is largely anecdotal; clinical studies are limited. Some people experience negative side effects including mood swings, depression, or weight gain. It is typically added after 6–12 months of estrogen therapy.

## What HRT Does (and Doesn't) Do

### Physical Changes

::: warning Timelines are highly individual
The ranges below are population-level observations, not predictions for any one person. Onset and progression depend heavily on your regimen, dosage, age, genetics, and overall health. The "typical plateau" column does **not** mean change stops at that point — many people continue to see changes beyond these timeframes, particularly with regimen adjustments. Do not use this table to judge your own progress.
:::

| Change | Typical Onset | Typical Plateau | Reversible? |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Breast development | 3–6 months | 2–3 years* | **No** |
| Skin softening / decreased oiliness | 3–6 months | Variable | **Yes** |
| Body fat redistribution (to hips/thighs) | 3–6 months | 2–5 years* | **Yes** (slowly) |
| Decreased muscle mass | 3–6 months | 1–2 years | **Yes** |
| Reduced body/facial hair growth | 6–12 months | 3+ years | **Partially** |
| Decreased libido / spontaneous erections | 1–3 months | 1–2 years | **Yes** |
| Decreased testicular volume | 3–6 months | 2–3 years | **Partially** |
| Scalp hair loss slowing/reversal | 1–3 months | 1–2 years | **Partially** |

\* Changes in diet, weight, exercise, or HRT regimen can continue to drive development beyond the typical plateau.

::: danger Fertility Warning
Feminising HRT will likely reduce your fertility, possibly permanently. HRT is **not** a contraceptive — use a reliable method of birth control if relevant to you.
:::

::: info Gamete Storage
If you are considering having biological children in the future, discuss sperm/gamete storage with a healthcare provider **before** starting HRT.
:::

### What HRT Does NOT Do

- **Change your voice** — Estrogen does not affect the vocal cords. Voice training or surgery is required.
- **Remove facial or body hair** — Estrogen slows hair growth but does not remove existing terminal hair. Laser hair removal or electrolysis is needed for permanent results.
- **Guarantee fertility preservation** — HRT significantly reduces sperm production; permanent infertility is possible.
- **Shrink your Adam's apple** — Estrogen does not affect cartilage. Surgical reduction (chondrolaryngoplasty) is the only option.

## Key Hormone Targets & Monitoring

For feminising GAHT, the Endocrine Society and WPATH SOC8 recommend targeting:
- **Testosterone (T)** — below **50 ng/dL (< 1.73 nmol/L)**
- **Estradiol (E2)** — in the cisgender female range, typically **100–200 pg/mL (367–734 pmol/L)**

Achieving both targets simultaneously is not always possible. [A Mount Sinai study of 1,195 trans feminine individuals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10554830/) found that:
- Nearly half (43%) of people with E2 below target (<100 pg/mL) still achieved T suppression below 50 ng/dL.
- 88.5% of those with E2 above 200 pg/mL achieved T suppression, but some still did not.

The Endocrine Society recommends monitoring hormone levels **every 3 months** during the first year of therapy, and adjusting doses accordingly.

## Common Medications for Feminising HRT

### Estrogen Options

| Form | Examples | VTE Risk | Notes |
|------|----------|----------|-------|
| Oral (pills) | Estradiol valerate, hemihydrate | Higher | 2–8 mg/day; can be taken sublingually for better absorption |
| Transdermal (gel/patches) | Oestrogel, patches (50–100 mcg twice weekly) | **Lower** | Preferred by guidelines; steady absorption, less liver impact |
| Injections | Estradiol valerate (EV), cypionate (EC) | Lower | EV 2–4 mg starting, up to 8 mg maintenance (every 5 days); EC 1–5 mg every 7 days |

::: tip Transdermal is preferred
Clinical guidelines recommend transdermal 17β-estradiol over oral formulations, particularly for individuals with elevated cardiovascular or clotting risk, due to its more favourable safety profile.
:::

### Anti-Androgen (Testosterone Blocker) Options

| Medication | Mechanism | Typical Dose | Notes |
|------------|-----------|--------------|-------|
| Spironolactone | Blocks androgen receptors; mild T suppression | 50–200 mg/day | Most common in US; monitor potassium levels, especially in those over 45 |
| Bicalutamide | Competitively binds androgen receptors | 25–50 mg/day | Does not lower T levels; blocks T from binding |
| Cyproterone Acetate (CPA) | Progestogen; suppresses T production | 10–12.5 mg/day | Effective but associated with meningioma risk at higher doses — see warning below |
| GnRH Agonists | Suppresses pituitary gonadotropins | Injection every 12 weeks | Most effective method; expensive and not always accessible |
| Finasteride | 5α-reductase inhibitor (blocks DHT conversion) | 1–5 mg/day | Primarily for scalp hair preservation; not a full anti-androgen |

::: warning Cyproterone Acetate (CPA) and Meningioma Risk
Higher cumulative doses of CPA are associated with a significantly increased risk of meningioma (a type of usually benign brain tumour), with the risk being dose- and duration-dependent. The risk increases particularly with long-term use at doses of 25 mg/day or higher. The current recommended dose for HRT has been lowered to **10–12.5 mg/day** to reduce this risk.

Note: The European Medicines Agency (EMA) 2020 PRAC ruling formally restricts **all medicines with daily CPA doses of 10 mg or more**, meaning 12.5 mg/day is within the restricted range, not a safe ceiling below regulatory concern. Readers using 10–12.5 mg/day should understand this and discuss cumulative dose monitoring with a provider if possible. CPA is contraindicated in anyone with a history of meningioma.
:::

::: info On Finasteride
Finasteride is not a primary testosterone blocker for transfeminine HRT. It mainly reduces DHT (dihydrotestosterone), which helps with scalp hair preservation and can modestly slow body-hair progression. It is sometimes used alongside a primary anti-androgen.
:::

### Monotherapy (Estrogen Alone)

Using only estradiol (without an anti-androgen) is called **monotherapy**. High-dose injectable or transdermal estradiol can suppress testosterone to female range on its own for some individuals, making monotherapy viable in those cases.

::: danger Oral Monotherapy Warning
Do **not** attempt monotherapy with oral estrogen pills alone. Oral estrogen at doses high enough to suppress testosterone carries a significantly elevated risk of blood clots (VTE). Monotherapy is only appropriate with injectable or high-dose transdermal estradiol.
:::

## Side Effects & Risks

### Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) — Blood Clots

The most serious known adverse effect of feminising hormone therapy is **venous thromboembolism (VTE)** — the formation of blood clots in veins.

- Oral estrogen increases VTE risk by producing procoagulant changes in the liver — including increased APC resistance and elevated clotting factors IX and XI — because it undergoes first-pass hepatic metabolism. <sup>[[1]](#ref-1)</sup>
- Transdermal estradiol does not undergo first-pass metabolism and is not associated with the same procoagulant changes, making it the lower-risk option. <sup>[[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2)</sup>
- VTE risk is highest in the first year of hormone therapy and remains elevated thereafter. <sup>[[1]](#ref-1)</sup>
- Higher-risk groups include: people over 60, smokers, individuals with obesity, those with a personal or family history of VTE, and those with inherited clotting disorders (e.g. Factor V Leiden, antithrombin deficiency). <sup>[[2]](#ref-2)</sup>

### Other Side Effects

- **Blood pressure:** Estrogen therapy has been associated with increases in systolic blood pressure in some studies, making routine cardiovascular monitoring important. The relationship is complex and not consistent across all studies. <sup>[[3]](#ref-3)</sup>
- **Triglycerides:** Oral estrogen in particular has been associated with increased triglyceride levels, which may contribute to cardiovascular risk. <sup>[[4]](#ref-4)</sup>
- **Gallbladder disease:** Estrogen appears to promote gallstone formation by increasing cholesterol saturation in bile. A large study of over 38,000 trans women found a significantly increased risk of gallbladder disease in those on estrogen GAHT. <sup>[[5]](#ref-5)</sup>
- **Breast cancer:** Trans women on hormone therapy have a higher risk of breast cancer than cisgender men, but a lower risk than cisgender women. The 2019 Dutch nationwide cohort study (n = 2,260) found the risk was roughly 47× higher than cisgender men, but more than 3× lower than cisgender women. <sup>[[6]](#ref-6)</sup>
- **Elevated prolactin:** Cyproterone acetate and higher estradiol exposure have been associated with elevated prolactin levels in some patients, which should be monitored periodically. <sup>[[2]](#ref-2)</sup>

### Risk Mitigation

- **Use transdermal estradiol** if you have elevated cardiovascular or clotting risk — clinical guidelines strongly recommend this. <sup>[[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2)</sup>
- **Stop smoking** — smoking independently raises VTE and cardiovascular risk, and the combination with oral estrogen is particularly concerning. <sup>[[2]](#ref-2)</sup>
- **Maintain a healthy weight** and stay physically active to reduce baseline cardiovascular and thrombotic risk.
- **Monitor blood pressure and lipids** regularly as recommended by your provider. <sup>[[3]](#ref-3)</sup>
- **If taking spironolactone**, have potassium checked every 3 months in the first year, then annually. Risk of clinically significant hyperkalemia is low in otherwise healthy individuals under 45, but increases with age. <sup>[[7]](#ref-7)</sup> Note: while the Hayes et al. (2022) study suggests routine monitoring may be unnecessary in those under 45 without risk factors, the Endocrine Society's official guidelines still recommend 3-monthly monitoring for all patients — this is emerging evidence challenging current protocol, not yet the standard.
- **Before major surgery**, discuss with your provider whether to temporarily pause oral estrogen — practices vary between clinicians and institutions. <sup>[[1]](#ref-1)</sup>

## Is HRT Safe?

The Endocrine Society and other major medical organisations recognise gender-affirming hormone therapy as an evidence-based treatment for gender dysphoria when prescribed and monitored appropriately.

Research consistently demonstrates that GAHT is associated with improvements in psychological well-being, quality of life, and reductions in gender dysphoria. As with any long-term hormone therapy, ongoing monitoring is important to manage risk.

## How Long Until Changes Are Complete?

Physical changes are typically **fully apparent within 3–5 years**. Individual results vary based on:
- Dosage and route of administration
- Age — younger individuals often see faster changes
- Genetics and overall health

## DIY vs Prescribed HRT

### Prescribed HRT

- Under supervision of an endocrinologist or knowledgeable provider
- Regular monitoring and dose adjustments
- **Pros:** safer, medically supervised; free or low-cost in many healthcare systems
- **Cons:** long waiting lists (up to 5–7 years in some countries), gatekeeping barriers, high costs in private systems

### DIY HRT

- Obtaining medications directly without a prescription
- **Pros:** immediate access, full control over your care, often cheaper
- **Cons:** requires self-education and personal responsibility for sourcing and blood testing; no medical oversight

::: warning If you use DIY HRT, you must:
1. **Test your blood regularly** — at minimum every 3 months during the first year
2. **Source from reputable vendors** with verified product quality
3. **Educate yourself thoroughly** — understand dosing, side effects, and emergency warning signs
:::

## Is HRT Reversible?

**Some effects are permanent or long-lasting:**
- **Breast development** — permanent once breast tissue forms (may reduce slightly if HRT stops)
- **Fertility changes** — may be permanent even after stopping HRT
- **Testicular atrophy** — partially reversible but can be long-lasting

**Most other changes reverse if HRT is stopped:**
- Fat redistribution, skin softening, reduced muscle mass, and other hormonal effects generally reverse over time, though this can take months to years.
